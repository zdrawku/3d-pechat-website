# 3D Printed Spare Parts: How to Order a Discontinued Part

**Reading time: 9 min**

We have all been there. The appliance works perfectly, but one small plastic part has broken. The dishwasher knob, the blind clip, the vacuum cleaner cap, the fridge shelf holder. The manufacturer dropped the model long ago, the shops don't stock the part, and you are about to throw away a whole appliance because of a bit of plastic.

This is where **3D printing** is a practical fix. With a modern **3D printer**, a single part can be made from a broken sample, a photo or measurements, with no mould and no minimum quantity. In this article we cover what to measure and photograph, when FDM printing in PLA is a good fit and when it isn't, how long it all takes and how an order works with us.

## Why 3D printed spare parts make sense

Plastic parts are traditionally made by injection moulding. It needs an expensive mould that only pays off over thousands of pieces. That is why manufacturers stop making parts for older models and simply list them as out of stock.

3D printing needs no mould. The part is built layer by layer straight from a digital model. This means that:

- you can order **a single piece**;
- the cost depends mainly on the weight of the part, not on complex tooling;
- the shape can be adjusted if the first version doesn't fit perfectly;
- the model can be saved and printed again if the part breaks a second time.

### What kinds of parts people usually want to restore

Most often these are parts that don't carry a heavy load:

- knobs, handles and rotary controls;
- caps, plugs and end covers;
- clips, brackets and cable holders;
- guides and supports in shelves, drawers and boxes;
- housing parts, such as a battery compartment cover;
- low-load gears and small wheels (with a caveat, covered below);
- brackets for a home or hobby device.

## What we need to make the part

An order can start from a **ready 3D model, a sketch, a photo or just an idea**. The more information you give us, the more accurate the result. The three most common cases are described below.

### Option 1: You have the broken sample

This is the best case. Even if the part is in two or three pieces, accurate dimensions can be taken from it. Keep every fragment, even tiny ones. Don't glue the pieces together unless you have to, as this can distort the outline.

### Option 2: You only have a photo

A photo helps, but on its own it isn't enough for an accurate size. It needs at least one reference dimension, such as a length or diameter measured with a ruler or calipers. Without one, the part may be scaled wrongly.

### Option 3: You have dimensions and a description

If you don't have the sample but you know where the part sits, it can be made from the dimensions of the place where it fits. Send the size of the hole, the shaft, the mounting and the distances to neighbouring parts. You can add a photo of the mounting spot too.

## What to measure

The best tool is a digital caliper. It is inexpensive and useful for plenty of jobs around the house. If you don't have one, use a straight ruler or graph paper, but accuracy will be lower.

Measure and write down:

- **overall length, width and height** of the part;
- **diameters of holes and shafts**, especially where another part fits;
- **wall thickness** at the thin spots;
- **distances between holes** (centre to centre);
- **thread size**, if there are threads. If you don't know the pitch, just say so;
- **angles**, if they matter for the fit;
- **which surfaces are working surfaces**, i.e. where the part touches another one.

> **Tip:** Measure every critical dimension twice and write it down straight away. An error of half a millimetre on a shaft or hole is enough to stop the part from fitting.

### What to photograph

Photos help us understand the shape and function of the part. Take:

1. Photos from the front, back, top, bottom and both sides of the part.
2. Close-ups of all smaller features: slots, tabs, ribs, threads.
3. A photo of the part next to a ruler or calipers, so the scale is visible.
4. A photo of the place where the part fits, with the part removed if possible.
5. A photo of the appliance itself or its model label, if there is one.

Shoot on a light surface in good, even lighting without strong glare. This makes the edges and small details easier to see.

## When FDM printing in PLA is a good fit, and when it isn't

We print only with **FDM technology** on a Bambu Lab A1. It lays down melted filament in layers, and the main material we keep in stock is PLA. It is a practical, affordable material for many uses, but not for all of them.

### Good cases

PLA works well for parts that:

- are used indoors at room temperature;
- aren't exposed to hard impacts or constant flexing;
- are decorative, positional or lightly loaded;
- don't touch hot surfaces, a motor or a heater;
- aren't linked to food or to personal safety.

### Unsuitable or risky cases

Be careful when the part:

- sits near a heat source (a stove, an oven, a motor, a car interior in summer), because PLA softens at fairly low temperatures;
- is outdoors in direct sun and rain for a long time;
- is a load-bearing structure that safety depends on (parts for high-voltage appliances, wheels, brakes, climbing);
- has to be flexible and bend repeatedly;
- is in contact with chemicals, oils or solvents;
- is in contact with food or drinking water.

On request we can also use another material, such as **PETG** (more resistant to heat and moisture) or **TPU** (flexible). These are **not kept in stock**, so we buy them specially for your order. This can affect the lead time and the price, so we agree it with you in the quote.

If you are unsure which material to pick, see [PLA, PLA+, PLA Pro and PLN: Differences and How to Choose](/en/blog/pla-vs-pla-plus-vs-pla-pro-vs-pln/). SLA and SLS are only general information for us: we print exclusively with FDM.

### Quick guide: material by working conditions

| Working conditions | PLA (in stock) | PETG or TPU (on request) |
|---|---|---|
| Indoors, room temperature | Suitable | Also possible |
| Light load, positional parts | Suitable | Also possible |
| Heat and sun | Not recommended | PETG is the better choice |
| Moisture and outdoor use | Limited | PETG is the better choice |
| Flexibility and bending | Not suitable | TPU |
| Food contact or personal safety | Not recommended | Discussed case by case |

The table is a rough guide. The decision is made for the specific part, so describe the working conditions in your enquiry.

## Part strength and print settings

In FDM printing, strength depends not only on the material but also on the **print orientation**, wall thickness and **infill density**. Layers bond weakly in the vertical direction, so the part is oriented so that the main load doesn't pull the layers apart.

When a part needs to be stronger, the walls and infill are usually increased. You will find the details in [3D Printing Infill: Density, Strength and Filament Use](/en/blog/3d-printing-infill-density-strength/).

If the part is visible and you want a smoother surface, see [How to Smooth PLA Prints: 7 Methods That Work](/en/blog/how-to-smooth-pla-prints/). Keep in mind that sanding and smoothing can change dimensions slightly, so mention it up front if tolerances are tight.

## 3D modelling: what it involves and how the price is set

If you don't have a ready file, the part first has to be **modelled in 3D**. This is the most important step for spare parts, because it decides whether the part will fit.

The price of modelling **depends on the complexity of the part and the time needed to model it**. A simple cap with a hole takes little time, while a part with complex curves, ribs and threads takes more work. You learn the exact amount in the **quote, before we start**, and the first quote is free.

If you already have a model in STL, 3MF, OBJ or STEP, modelling isn't needed and only printing remains. For models downloaded from the internet, check that the dimensions match your sample. They are often made for a different version of the appliance.

## Common mistakes when ordering a spare part

- **Missing dimensions.** A photo alone, with no reference measurement, gives an inaccurate result.
- **Throwing away the sample.** Even the broken part is valuable information. Don't bin it until the new one is fitted.
- **Vague information about the load.** If you don't describe the conditions, an unsuitable material may be chosen.
- **Expecting an identical surface.** FDM prints show layer lines. If appearance matters, tell us in advance.
- **Too short a deadline.** Modelling and any corrections take time, so order early.
